Search for AI training in Faisalabad and you will find no shortage of options. Institute banners along Jaranwala Road, sponsored posts in local Facebook groups, WhatsApp forwards promising six-figure incomes.

Some of it is good. A lot of it is a recorded video library with a certificate attached.

This article is not a ranked list of local institutes — we teach here, so any ranking we published would be self-serving and you would be right to distrust it. Instead, this is the evaluation framework we would use ourselves, written as nine questions. Ask them of any provider, including us, and the answers will separate serious programmes from expensive ones very quickly.

## First, understand what you are buying

An AI course is not selling you information. Information is free and abundant. What a good course sells is **compression** — turning eighteen months of confused self-study into three months of directed effort — and **accountability**, which is the thing almost nobody supplies themselves.

Judge every programme against those two things. A course that gives you videos supplies neither. A course that gives you a trainer who reviews your work weekly supplies both.

## The nine questions

### 1. Is the instruction live, or recorded?

This is the fastest filter and it eliminates a surprising number of options.

Recorded content has a place — it is excellent for revision and for topics you already half understand. As a primary delivery method it fails most learners, because there is nobody to notice when you stop.

Ask directly: *are sessions live, and is the same trainer present every session?* Some programmes advertise "live support" that turns out to be a shared WhatsApp group with a response time measured in days.

At our institute every session in the [AI and Automation programme](/courses/ai-and-automation) is live, taught by the same trainer throughout, and recorded afterwards for revision — recordings supplement the class rather than replacing it.

### 2. What is the batch size?

Ask for the number. Then ask what the maximum is, because those are different questions.

The reason this matters is arithmetic. A trainer reviewing every student's weekly submission properly needs roughly fifteen to twenty minutes per student. At eighteen students that is around five hours a week — demanding but achievable. At forty students it is eleven hours, which nobody does, which means submissions get glanced at rather than critiqued.

Weekly critique is the mechanism that makes a course work. Without it you have an audience, not a class. We cap ours at eighteen for exactly this reason.

### 3. Does the syllabus include retrieval, or does it stop at prompting?

This is the most useful technical filter available to a non-technical buyer.

Prompt engineering is genuinely valuable and every course covers it. But a course that stops there has taught you something everyone will know within two years. The commercially durable skill is **retrieval-augmented generation** — connecting a model to a specific organisation's own documents so its answers are grounded in reality rather than invented.

That single capability is what turns a novelty chatbot into a support system a business will pay for monthly. If retrieval, embeddings and knowledge-base construction do not appear anywhere in the syllabus, the course is teaching the easy half.

Ask to see the full module list before you pay. Any institute that will not publish its syllabus is telling you something.

### 4. Do you build and deploy something a real person uses?

The single strongest predictor of whether a graduate earns is whether they finished with a deployed system that somebody other than the trainer actually used.

Not a demo. Not a notebook. A working thing, handed over, with the boring parts done: error handling, logging, cost control, and a document explaining how to run it.

Ask: *what exactly do I have at the end, and who uses it?* If the answer is "a certificate", keep looking.

### 5. Which automation platforms are taught?

Almost all real client work involves connecting AI to systems a business already uses — WhatsApp, email, spreadsheets, CRMs, ad platforms. Doing that from scratch means building and hosting a backend. Doing it on a platform like n8n or Make means shipping in days and handing over something the client can see and understand.

A course that teaches only API calls produces graduates who can prototype but struggle to deliver. Ask which platforms are covered and whether you build multi-step workflows with error handling, not just single connections.

### 6. Is pricing, proposals and handover part of the syllabus?

This is the question almost nobody asks and it separates courses that produce income from courses that produce knowledge.

Technical training that ends at "and now you know how to build it" leaves the hardest commercial part — finding the client, scoping the work, quoting a number without flinching, handing over so you are not on call forever — entirely to you.

Ask whether the syllabus includes pricing, proposals and client handover. If it does not, budget separately for a course that does. Our students frequently pair the AI track with [Freelancing Mastery](/courses/freelancing-mastery), and it is the pairing rather than either course alone that produces the results we see.

### 7. Who is the trainer, and what have they shipped?

Ask for a name. Then ask what they have built for paying clients in the last year.

This is not gatekeeping. AI moves fast enough that a trainer who is not actively deploying systems is teaching from memory, and memory in this field is stale within months. Someone doing the work knows which things break, which vendors have changed pricing, and which approaches quietly stopped working — and none of that reaches a static syllabus.

A trainer with genuine current practice will answer this question happily. Hesitation is informative.

### 8. What are the actual outcomes from the last three batches?

Ask for numbers, not adjectives.

- How many students enrolled?
- How many completed?
- How many are earning within six months, and in what capacity?
- Can I speak to two graduates?

An institute that tracks its outcomes can answer immediately. One that does not will offer testimonials instead, which are selected by definition.

For the record, ours across all programmes: **92% completion, 76% earning within six months**. Not 100%, because that number would be fictional. We publish the figures on our [Why Choose Globify](/why-choose-us) page and will introduce you to graduates on request.

### 9. What does the fee actually cover?

Get the total, in writing, before you commit. Specifically ask about:

- Registration or admission charges added on top
- Course materials
- Access to paid AI tools and API credits during the course
- Assessment and certificate
- Whether lab access is included outside class hours
- Refund terms if you withdraw in the first two weeks

The last one matters most. A programme confident in its quality will offer a genuine early-withdrawal refund. One that will not is protecting itself against something.

| Small-batch project programme | Rs 25,000 – 50,000 | Live teaching, weekly critique, deployed capstone, career support |

Cheaper is not automatically worse — a recorded library is excellent value if you already know you can self-direct. But be clear about which product you are buying. Most people who fail at AI self-study do not fail on content access. They fail on sequence and follow-through, and those are exactly what the third row supplies.

## Red flags worth walking away from

- **Guaranteed income or guaranteed job claims.** Nobody can honestly make these. An institute that does is telling you about its integrity, not its results.
- **No published syllabus.** If you cannot see the module list before paying, assume it is thin.
- **"Learn AI in 7 days."** You can learn to use a chatbot in seven days. You cannot become employable.
- **Heavy focus on model theory.** Interesting, and almost entirely irrelevant to the work available to you now.
- **No named trainer.** If the marketing never names who teaches, ask why.
- **Pressure to pay immediately.** Urgency about a genuine deadline is fine. Pressure that prevents you from asking questions is not.

## Before you decide anything

Two pieces of advice that apply regardless of where you study.

**Do not enrol in AI as your first ever technical subject if you have never finished a course before.** Start with something shorter — [Office Automation](/courses/office-automation) or [Canva Mastery](/courses/canva-mastery) — and prove to yourself you can complete a programme. Confidence built on a finished four-week course transfers. Confidence built on nothing does not.

**Do not choose AI because it is the most impressive-sounding option.** Choose it because the daily work suits you: methodical problem-solving, a lot of reading documentation, and a fair amount of debugging things that should work but do not.
